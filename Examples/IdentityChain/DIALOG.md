# Fugue on a Single Seal

The Tortoise, true to his word, has brought Achilles across town to a different house. The party inside looks much like the first — tags, notebooks, dance cards, the periodic ripple of shoulder-taps — but every guest here also carries a small personal quill, and pinned inside each notebook's cover is not one letter but a little sheaf of them, fastened end to end.

Achilles: Another endless party! And I see the same wax impressions gathering guests into someones. So the Anteater — there, and there, and there, three bodies of him — keeps a signet ring in a drawer, and carries it round to stamp each new body's letter?

Tortoise: Look closer at the letters, Achilles. Take that third Anteater's notebook and read what's pinned in the cover.

Achilles: It's... a sheaf. The first letter bears the seal, and says: I vouch that my first body, wearing such-and-such a tag and writing with such-and-such a quill, is mine. The second letter is not sealed — it's merely signed, in ink, by that first body's quill, vouching for a second body. And the third is signed by the second body's quill, vouching for this third one!

Tortoise: A pedigree. In this house, the ring stamped exactly one letter in its life — the founding one — and then went back in its drawer, where it might as well be melted down. Everything after is quillwork: each enrolled body may vouch for the next, and the next, and the next. The seal is an ancestor now, not a clerk.

Achilles: Then when a new body wishes to join —

Tortoise: It writes a request card: its tag, and the public nib-pattern of its quill. Nothing secret — you could read the card aloud at dinner and lose nothing. Any enrolled body copies its own sheaf, adds one letter vouching for the requester, and hands the whole thing back. The newcomer pins it in, replacing the one-letter sheaf it wrote for itself the day it walked in alone.

Achilles: No brown-paper parcels! No ring rattling across town in a coat pocket! Mr. Tortoise, this is strictly better than the Crab's arrangement — why would anyone carry the ring?

Tortoise: Because of what a reader must now do, and what a thief can now become. When a sheaf arrives in your notebook, you cannot glance at one seal and be done. You must walk it: every signature sound, every letter's author the very body the previous letter vouched for, and the final letter naming the tag that actually tapped you — that last check, as before, is what keeps a guest from pinning a stolen pedigree in his own cover. And consider: every quill along the path vouched for the next. Trust runs through the whole procession. Steal one quill, and until something is done about it, the thief can vouch impostors into the family — a whole false branch, grafted with honest-looking ink.

Achilles: Until something is done. What is done?

Tortoise: (producing a black-bordered notice) This. A burning. A signed declaration that reads, in full: this quill is burned. Notices are copied into every notebook and passed along with everything else, and the pile of them only grows — no notice is ever torn up, no quill un-burned. A body that recovers its wits after a theft doesn't plead reinstatement; it takes a fresh quill and is vouched in anew.

Achilles: But wait — anyone may write such a notice? Then I could burn the Anteater's quill this instant, out of spite!

Tortoise: Write it, and every notebook will dutifully carry your spite to every corner of the party — and no reader will care. A notice is weighed only when a pedigree is read, and it counts against a sheaf only if its signer sits earlier on that same sheaf — the seal, or an ancestor quill on the path. Authority flows downward, Achilles, never up and never sideways. A parent may disown what it vouched for. A stolen leaf cannot burn the tree that bore it. Brothers cannot feud. And a burned quill's own notices die with its place in the line — cut the branch, and its curses fall with it.

Achilles: And if a body in the middle of a pedigree is burned?

Tortoise: Then every sheaf running through it is severed at that letter. Its children, its children's children — all of them, back to strangers under Unverified, however honest their ink. When you burn a bough, you burn its blossoms.

Achilles: One thing troubles me about the timing. News crawls through this house notebook by notebook. Suppose a notice is written tonight but reaches me Thursday — and meanwhile the burned quill has been merrily vouching?

Tortoise: Then until Thursday, you are deceived, and no cleverness in this house prevents it. That is why no reader ever marks a pedigree valid in permanent ink — the judgment is made fresh each time the sheaf is read, against whatever notices have arrived by then. A pedigree that read true on Monday may read severed on Thursday, cut by a notice older than your knowledge of it. The question in a house of chains is never is this link broken but did I yet hear which link broke first.

Achilles: And the founder? If the founding body's quill is stolen — the one the seal itself vouched for?

Tortoise: Then the thief outranks every quill in the family, and no one outranks the thief. The dynasty is lost, Achilles — wholly, permanently. Other houses answer that with anchors driven outside the party altogether, or seals that rotate. This house does neither; it merely refuses to pretend otherwise, which is a kind of honesty I've come to admire.

Achilles: (subdued) It seems a heavy machinery for a party. I'm glad I am merely a guest here, and not a dynasty.

Tortoise: Merely a guest? Achilles — the moment you stepped through that door, you wrote yourself a one-letter sheaf with a seal of your own. Every guest here is a founding ancestor. Most simply die childless.
