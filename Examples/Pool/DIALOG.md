# Ricercar for One Voice

Upstairs from the party, Achilles discovers a study: one desk, many doors, and — scattered across the house — several identical notebooks, each stamped THE NOTEBOOK in gold. The Tortoise is writing in one of them.

Achilles: Mr. Tortoise! I've found you at last. But tell me — I passed a notebook just like that one in the garden, and another by the front door. Which is the real one?

Tortoise: All of them.

Achilles: Come now. If I write "the sky is falling" in the garden copy, the one under your pen knows nothing of it.

Tortoise: Not yet. But whenever two of my notebooks meet, they pour into one another until neither can tell which page came from where. Every entry is born with a little seal — struck once, never re-struck — so an entry arriving twice by different doors settles into place just once. Downstairs at the party, every guest signs their stories. Up here nobody signs anything. Why would I? Every hand in this house is mine.

Achilles: One author, many pens. But suppose the pens disagree! Say you amend a line in the garden while the study copy still holds the old wording. When they meet, which survives?

Tortoise: The newer ink. I date every stroke, and when copies meet, each seal keeps only its freshest line.

Achilles: And if two amendments carry the very same date, to the minute?

Tortoise: Then the alphabet arbitrates. An arbitrary judge, I grant you — but the same arbitrary judge in every room, and that is the whole trick. It matters not a whit which copy meets which, or in what order. Ask any two of my notebooks the same question and you get the same answer, eventually, always.

Achilles: You said "eventually." The garden is a long way from the attic. Must they meet directly?

Tortoise: Never necessarily. Watch the edition stamp on the cover. It ticks whenever a notebook changes — and hear me carefully, Achilles: learning counts as changing. When the study copy absorbs the garden's news, its own edition ticks, though I wrote nothing at that desk. So the hallway copy, seeing the study's stamp advance, drinks from it in turn, and the garden's news arrives in the attic by way of rooms the garden has never seen.

Achilles: So a notebook that merely overhears becomes a teller itself! Downstairs they recite only their own stories, and keep everyone else's in the back pages, forever filed by guest.

Tortoise: Whereas up here there are no back pages. One book, one text, told whole by every copy. The party is a chorus of soloists; this is a ricercar for one voice, played on however many instruments I happen to own.

Achilles: May I test it? (He writes "Achilles was here" in the desk copy, then sprints to the garden and back.) The garden copy has it already — seal, date, and all! And its edition has ticked, though the garden never saw my pen.

Tortoise: And should you now amend it in the attic to "Achilles was briefly here," every copy in the house will shortly agree that your visit was brief. The house keeps no memory of the argument — only the newest ink survives.

Achilles: One thing troubles me still. I made an error earlier — a line I'd rather no copy carried. I tore the page from the hallway copy, yet the line came back by evening, fresh as ever.

Tortoise: Of course it did. To every other copy, a missing line is merely news that hasn't arrived yet, and they helpfully supply it. Nothing here can be unwritten, Achilles. If you truly wish a line gone, you must do a stranger thing: write down the erasing — keep the seal, and under it record "this line is dead" — so the death itself can spread from room to room like any other news.

Achilles: A gravestone in every copy, so no copy plays the resurrectionist. And until I write such epitaphs?

Tortoise: Until then, the house grants only two powers, and grants them everywhere: say something new, or say something better. It is a small grammar. But observe — (he gestures at the notebooks, all quietly agreeing with one another) — how much of a house it furnishes.
