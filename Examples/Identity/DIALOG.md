# Aria with Diverse Impressions

Achilles and the Tortoise, at the same unending party. Achilles has just been tapped on the shoulder by the Crab — who is also, at that very moment, plainly visible on the far side of the room, tapping someone else.

Achilles: Mr. Tortoise — the Crab has just greeted me, and yet there he is by the punch bowl. One of these Crabs is an impostor!

Tortoise: Or neither is. Read their tags.

Achilles: Different tags entirely! Which proves my point — the house forges each tag once, so two tags mean two guests.

Tortoise: Two guests, certainly. But open the notebook the nearer Crab just traded you, and look inside the front cover.

Achilles: There's a little letter pinned there. "The bearer of this tag is one of mine. Issue No. 2." And below that, a wax impression — quite an ornate one.

Tortoise: Now the punch-bowl Crab's notebook. You have it already; everyone does.

Achilles: Another letter... a different tag named, Issue No. 1 — but the same wax impression! The very same seal!

Tortoise: And there is your answer. Somewhere across town, in a drawer, lies the Crab's signet ring. The ring never comes to the party — it doesn't tap, doesn't trade, doesn't wear a tag. Its one talent is stamping letters that say this guest is mine. The tags are bodies, Achilles. The seal is the Crab.

Achilles: Then when my notebook's index lists the guests I've heard from —

Tortoise: — it needn't list a rabble of tags. It gathers them under their seals: here the Crab, twice embodied; there the Sloth, once; and at the bottom, under Unverified, whoever showed up with no letter at all.

Achilles: Hold on. Letters ride along in notebooks, and notebooks are copied to everyone. What stops me from copying the Crab's letter into my own front cover and being filed as a Crab?

Tortoise: Read the letter again, closely. It names the bearer's tag — and the house never lies to you about whose tap you just received. Were you to pin the Crab's letter in your cover, your reader would see a letter naming a tag that isn't yours, tear it out on the spot, and keep your stories anyway. You'd sync perfectly well. You'd simply be filed under Unverified, where tag-wearers with no proof belong.

Achilles: Very well — but then how did the second Crab body come by a letter? The ring is in a drawer across town!

Tortoise: Ah. It wasn't always. When the Crab took his second lodging, he wrapped the ring in brown paper and carried it there, and the ring stamped a fresh letter on arrival. That is the whole enrollment ceremony: the ring travels, the ring stamps, the ring goes back in a drawer — now two drawers, really.

Achilles: But Mr. Tortoise, that's dreadful! Anyone who lifts that parcel in transit doesn't impersonate the Crab — they are the Crab! Every drawer that holds the ring is the whole Crab, ready to be burgled!

Tortoise: Entirely so, and the Crab knows it; carrying the ring is a solemn errand, done rarely and by hand. It buys him something, though: any guest can verify any letter with one glance at one seal. No pedigrees to trace, no genealogies of stamps. There is another party — I'll take you someday — where the ring never leaves its drawer, and authority travels instead as a chain of stamped letters, each vouching for the next. But chains raise delicate questions, such as which link was broken first, and that evening deserves its own telling.

Achilles: One more thing puzzles me. Why do the letters carry issue numbers?

Tortoise: Because a seal may stamp a fresh letter for the same tag, and the higher number quietly retires the lower. Nothing hangs on that nail yet, Achilles — but a nail driven in the right place is half of revocation, and the Crab drove it on purpose.

Achilles: So to summon his second self, the Crab carried the ring, stamped the letter — and was done?

Tortoise: Not quite done. A letter is not an introduction! The new body still walked in tagless-friendless, and had to tap its way onto dance cards like anyone. Enrolled is not connected. Only once the two bodies had traded notebooks did each discover, pinned in the other's cover, its own seal — and file its brother under mine.

Achilles: (slowly) Mr. Tortoise... which are you? The body before me, or the seal in a drawer somewhere?

Tortoise: Check your notebook.

Achilles: Your seal, Issue No. 1... but Mr. Tortoise, the seal is just wax! It tells me these bodies are the same someone — it doesn't tell me that someone is you!

Tortoise: Bravo, Achilles. A seal gathers; it does not christen. Matching names to wax is a matter for another evening entirely.
