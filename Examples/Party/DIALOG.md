# Sweeping Canon

Achilles and the Tortoise find themselves at a party in a rambling old house. The party appears to have no host, no beginning, and — as the Tortoise will insist — no end. Guests drift from room to room carrying small notebooks. Every so often, the whole room ripples with a round of shoulder-tapping, like a wave through a stadium.

Achilles: What a curious gathering, Mr. Tortoise. I've been here ten minutes and nobody has asked my name.

Tortoise: Nobody needs to. It's on your tag.

Achilles: But I never wrote a tag!

Tortoise: The house forged one for you the moment you first stepped in. Look down. That tag is yours forever — if you ever lost it, the guest who walked back in wouldn't be you, merely someone standing where you used to stand.

Achilles: How grim! And these notebooks everyone clutches?

Tortoise: Each guest writes only their own stories in the front. The back pages hold copies of everyone else's. Would you like to hear mine? (clears throat) Once upon a time—

Achilles: Wait — you told me your stories not ten minutes ago, in the coatroom!

Tortoise: And I shall tell you all of them again, from the very first word. That is the etiquette here. No guest ever says "here's what's new since last time." We hand over the whole notebook, every time.

Achilles: That seems monstrously wasteful. Suppose your notebook were a thousand pages!

Tortoise: Ah, but observe the stamp on the cover: Edition 12. Every time I add so much as a line, the number ticks up. You have my Edition 12 already, so my recitation would tell you nothing, and you may politely stop me. But were I to say "Edition 13" — then you'd throw out everything of mine you had, and take the new copy whole. It is a crude sort of memory, Achilles, but crudeness is the charm: nobody must remember what anybody else has heard. The number remembers for them.

(A wave of tapping sweeps the room. The Tortoise taps three guests in turn, consulting a small card.)

Achilles: And that card?

Tortoise: My dance card — the friends I've resolved to check in on. Twice a minute or so, I make my rounds and tap each one. A tap is nothing but a greeting; the notebook-trading follows of its own accord.

(A Crab whom Achilles has never met taps him on the shoulder.)

Achilles: Excuse me — do I know you? (The Crab wanders off.) How rude! Who was that?

Tortoise: Check your dance card.

Achilles: Great Zeus — he's on it now! I never added him!

Tortoise: In this house, being greeted is the same as being introduced. The Crab put you on his card, tapped you, and by that very tap put himself on yours. Next sweep, you'll tap him back, and his stories will settle into your back pages without your ever having asked. It is how the party knits itself together: friendship here travels in one direction and arrives in both.

Achilles: Then any story, told anywhere in the house, eventually washes up in every notebook?

Tortoise: So long as the taps keep coming, yes. Like a canon, Achilles — every voice sings the selfsame melody, merely offset in time, and given enough measures the whole round is heard in every part. Which reminds me: most guests here are dreadful company. They walk in, do exactly one thing — announce themselves, add a friend, read a single story into the room — and leave before the echo fades.

Achilles: Does anyone stay?

Tortoise: One. The watcher, by the wall — the quiet fellow who says nothing and writes nothing, but reads the whole room aloud each time any notebook changes. And note well what the house does all evening: it forges tags, keeps cards, carries envelopes, answers taps. It never once reads a story. The envelopes are sealed; what's inside is entirely the guests' affair.

Achilles: Mr. Tortoise, a disquieting thought has come over me. Your stories are in my back pages now. When I tap someone — anyone — they'll receive them too, won't they? And I notice this very conversation has become one of your stories.

Tortoise: Edition 13, Achilles. Do pass it along.
